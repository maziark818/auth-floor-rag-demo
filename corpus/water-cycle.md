# The Water Cycle

The water cycle is the continuous movement of water between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere, driven almost entirely by energy from the sun. Solar heating evaporates water from oceans, lakes, and soil, and plants release additional water vapor through transpiration. Together these processes lift enormous quantities of water into the air as an invisible gas.

As moist air rises it cools, and the water vapor condenses onto microscopic particles called condensation nuclei, forming the droplets that make up clouds. When droplets grow heavy enough, gravity pulls them back to the surface as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on the temperature profile of the air they fall through.

Water that reaches the ground follows several paths. Some runs off across the surface into streams and rivers, eventually returning to the ocean. Some infiltrates into the soil and percolates downward to recharge groundwater aquifers, where it may remain stored for days or for thousands of years. A watershed, also called a drainage basin, is the full area of land that drains into a single river system.

The cycle has no beginning and no end, and the total amount of water on Earth stays nearly constant. What changes is where the water resides and in what form: ocean, ice cap, aquifer, cloud, or living tissue. Residence times vary wildly, from about nine days in the atmosphere to millennia in deep groundwater or Antarctic ice.
