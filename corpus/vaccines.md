# How Vaccines Train the Immune System

A vaccine works by showing the immune system a harmless version of a pathogen, or a recognizable piece of one, so the body can learn to defeat the real thing later. The vaccine might contain an inactivated virus, a weakened live strain, or a single protein from the pathogen's surface. None of these can cause the full disease in a healthy person, but all carry the molecular patterns, called antigens, that the immune system uses for identification.

When antigen-presenting cells display these patterns, helper T cells coordinate a response and B cells begin producing antibodies, proteins shaped to bind precisely to the antigen. Some of the activated B and T cells become memory cells, which persist for years or decades. Immunological memory is the entire point of vaccination: the first encounter with a new pathogen takes days of costly trial and error, but a remembered one is met within hours.

If the vaccinated person later meets the actual pathogen, memory cells trigger a secondary response that is faster and far stronger than the primary response, typically destroying the invader before symptoms develop. When enough of a population carries this immunity, transmission chains break and even people who cannot be vaccinated gain protection, an effect known as herd immunity.

Booster doses exist because memory fades at different rates for different diseases, and because some pathogens, influenza above all, mutate their surface proteins so quickly that last year's antigen is no longer a reliable portrait of this year's virus.
