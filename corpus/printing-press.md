# The Printing Press and Movable Type

In the fifteenth century, Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing system in Mainz, Germany, that combined several existing technologies into something new: a hand press adapted from wine and paper making, oil-based ink that adhered to metal, and, crucially, movable type cast in a durable metal alloy. Individual letters could be arranged into a page, printed, then redistributed and reused for the next page.

Before movable type, books were copied by hand. A single Bible might take a team of scribes years to produce, and a book could cost as much as a house. Within decades of Gutenberg's workshop opening, printing presses spread to hundreds of European cities, and the price of books collapsed. By 1500, presses had produced millions of copies of thousands of titles, more books than all the scribes of the previous millennium.

Cheaper books changed who could read and what reading was for. Standardized texts made it possible for scholars in different cities to cite the same page of the same edition, accelerating science. Printed pamphlets and vernacular Bibles carried religious and political arguments far beyond the pulpit and the court, fueling the Reformation and, later, the Enlightenment.

Printing was not invented in Europe from nothing: woodblock printing had flourished in China for centuries, and Korean printers cast metal movable type before Gutenberg. Gutenberg's contribution was the integrated system, the type-casting mold above all, that made mass production economical at scale.
