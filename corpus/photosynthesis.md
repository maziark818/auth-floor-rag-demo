# Photosynthesis: How Plants Make Food

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. Inside plant cells, the pigment chlorophyll, housed in organelles called chloroplasts, absorbs sunlight most strongly in the blue and red parts of the spectrum and reflects green light, which is why most leaves appear green.

The process unfolds in two stages. In the light-dependent reactions, water molecules are split, releasing oxygen as a byproduct, and the captured energy is stored temporarily in the carrier molecules ATP and NADPH. In the second stage, the Calvin cycle, the plant uses that stored energy to fix carbon dioxide from the air into organic molecules, assembling glucose through a repeating series of enzyme-driven steps that do not directly require light.

The overall equation is simple to state: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, powered by light, yield one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. Nearly all life on Earth depends on this transformation, directly or indirectly, since almost every food chain begins with photosynthetic organisms, and the oxygen in the atmosphere was produced by photosynthesis over billions of years.

Rates of photosynthesis respond to the environment. Brighter light and higher carbon dioxide concentration speed the process up to a saturation point, while water scarcity forces plants to close their stomata, the pores that admit carbon dioxide, slowing growth even under a bright sun.
