# How Sourdough Fermentation Works

A sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria kept alive in a mixture of flour and water. Unlike bread made with commercial yeast, sourdough rises because the wild yeast in the starter produces carbon dioxide gas as it feeds on the sugars in the flour. The gas is trapped by the gluten network in the dough, and the loaf slowly expands.

The tangy flavor that defines sourdough comes from the bacteria, not the yeast. Lactobacillus species convert sugars into lactic acid and acetic acid. Lactic acid tastes mild and yogurt-like, while acetic acid is sharper and more vinegary. A warmer, wetter starter favors lactic acid; a cooler, stiffer one produces more acetic acid, so bakers steer flavor by adjusting temperature and hydration.

Feeding the starter on a regular schedule keeps the culture in balance. Each feeding dilutes the accumulated acid and gives the microbes fresh starch to eat. If a starter is neglected, acid builds up until it begins to break down the gluten and the yeast population weakens. A healthy starter doubles in size within a few hours of feeding and smells pleasantly fruity rather than harsh.

During the long bulk fermentation, enzymes also go to work. Amylase breaks starch into maltose and glucose, which feed the microbes, and proteases partially break down gluten proteins. This is why long-fermented sourdough is often easier to digest, and why over-proofed dough turns slack: given too much time, the proteases dismantle the very structure that traps the gas.
