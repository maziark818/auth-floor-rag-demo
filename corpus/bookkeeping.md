# Basics of Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Double-entry bookkeeping is a system for recording financial transactions in which every transaction affects at least two accounts. Each entry has a debit side and a credit side, and the total of all debits must always equal the total of all credits. This equality is a built-in error check: if the books do not balance, a recording mistake has been made somewhere.

Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on the account type. Debits increase asset and expense accounts, while credits increase liability, equity, and revenue accounts. When a business sells goods for cash, for example, the cash account (an asset) is debited and the sales revenue account is credited for the same amount.

Transactions are first recorded chronologically in a journal, then posted to the ledger, which organizes all entries by account. At the end of an accounting period, the ledger balances are summarized in a trial balance, a listing used to verify that total debits still equal total credits before financial statements are prepared.

The system rests on the accounting equation: assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity. Every transaction preserves this equation, which is why the balance sheet, the statement that reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time, is called a balance sheet. Double-entry methods were documented in Venice in the fifteenth century and remain the foundation of accounting worldwide.
